Study Finds Average Meeting Could Have Been Email and Also Wasn't

Researchers describe paradox as 'the Schrödinger stand-up.

By Dr. Helen Marsh · Saturday, July 11, 2026 · 1 min read

BOSTON — MIT researchers published a paper Monday demonstrating that 68% of workplace meetings simultaneously could and could not have been emails until observed by a manager entering the room.

"The moment leadership joins, the meeting collapses into necessity," said lead author Dr. Stephen Voss. "Before that, it's quantum fluff."

The team instrumented 200 conference rooms with microphones and guilt sensors. They detected peak productivity during the two minutes before start time when attendees almost sent the email but didn't, fearing it seemed rude.

Findings explain why calendars fill with recurring syncs that exist "just to align," yet never align. Voss proposed replacing half with structured documents and the other half with honest admission that people like hearing themselves talk.

Corporate clients resisted. "Email lacks synergy," said CloudPath executive Dana Hooper, scheduling a meeting to discuss the study.

The paper introduced the Schrödinger stand-up: a daily ritual where teams report blockers that were blockers only because nobody replied in Slack.

Voss recommended a simple test: if the meeting lacks decisions, food, or legal requirement, send the email and touch grass per CDC guidance.

He acknowledged the advice may itself require a meeting to implement.
