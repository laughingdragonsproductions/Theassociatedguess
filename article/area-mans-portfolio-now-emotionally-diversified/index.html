Area Man's Portfolio Now Emotionally Diversified

Financial advisor recommends balancing regret, cautious hope, and one meme stock 'for the plot.

By Marcus Tweed · Wednesday, August 19, 2026 · NORWALK · 3 min read

Share Email Post Reprints

NORWALK - After three years of checking his retirement account “just to see if it was still there,” area man Owen Kessler announced Tuesday that his portfolio is finally emotionally diversified, a milestone his advisor described as “more stable than the actual numbers.” Kessler’s journey from constant anxiety to a more balanced emotional state has been anything but smooth.

“I’ve got 40% in index funds, 30% in bonds, 20% in cash, and 10% in a company I don’t understand but liked the logo,” Kessler said. “Emotionally, though, I’m spread across mild panic, performative calm, and one position I refuse to discuss at Thanksgiving.” His advisor, Lila Gant, certified financial planner, explained that emotional diversification protects investors from putting all their anxiety in one basket.

“Owen used to refresh his app every four minutes,” she said. “Now he refreshes every six, and only on green days, which we call growth.” Kessler’s largest holding remains a broad market ETF, which he refers to as “the thing I pretend I chose on purpose.” His smallest position is a cryptocurrency named after a dog that died in 2011, which he bought “because a guy on a podcast sounded confident, and I miss having hobbies.”

Behavioral economists say many Americans now treat investing less as wealth building and more as a subscription to suspense. “The market opens at nine-thirty and delivers content,” said Dr. Priya Nand. “Some people pay Netflix. Owen pays volatility.” Kessler plans to rebalance quarterly, or whenever he reads a headline containing the word “unprecedented.” His advisor recommended setting alerts for price targets, not feelings. Kessler said he would think about it after lunch, then checked his phone twice before finishing the sentence.

Local color from financial advisors and investors in Norwalk echoed Kessler’s experience. Sarah Thompson, a financial planner with a local firm, noted that many clients are now treating their portfolios like reality TV shows - full of twists and turns. “We’re seeing more people who want to diversify not just their investments but their emotional responses,” she said.

At the Norwalk Stock Exchange, where a replica of the New York Stock Exchange building stands as a tourist attraction, visitors were seen taking photos with a life-size cutout of Kessler, whose name is now plastered on the wall. “It’s like he’s a local celebrity here,” said tourist Mike Johnson from Cleveland. “I mean, who doesn’t want to be diversified emotionally?”

Kessler’s journey has not been without its challenges. His wife, Jane, a high school teacher, noticed the change in his behavior and commented, “He used to get so worked up over every little dip or spike. Now he just says, ‘It’s all good, honey,’ even when it’s clearly not.” Kessler admitted that while he still checks his portfolio frequently, he now has a mantra: “Don’t panic, don’t perform.”

The Norwalk Economic Development Board is considering naming the new downtown park after Kessler, citing his innovative approach to financial wellness. “We’re looking at Owen as an example of how local residents can lead by example,” said board member Emily White.

Kessler’s advisor, Lila Gant, emphasized that while emotional diversification might seem like a novel concept, it’s crucial for long-term mental health and financial stability. “Owen is a perfect case study - his portfolio mirrors his emotional state perfectly now,” she said. “And who knows? Maybe he’ll start investing in more hobbies than cryptocurrencies.”

As Kessler walked out of the office with his advisor, he glanced at his phone one last time before putting it away. “I think I’m ready for a break from this suspense,” he said. “Maybe I’ll go get that hobby I’ve been talking about.”
