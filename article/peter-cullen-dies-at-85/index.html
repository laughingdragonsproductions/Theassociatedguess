Peter Cullen Dies at 85

The same voice told a generation to roll out and told Eeyore the day was probably going to be a disaster. Both times, it sounded true.

By The Associated Guess Staff · Thursday, August 27, 2026 · 3 min read

LOS ANGELES - Peter Cullen died Tuesday at his home. He was 85.

His family said he passed peacefully on the night of August 26, surrounded by people who loved him. No foul play is suspected.

There are actors you recognize on sight. Peter Cullen was the other kind. You knew him the way you know a weather report or a parent's footsteps on the stairs - by sound, by timing, by the instant calm that arrived when the right voice started talking.

If the name does not ring a bell, the work will. You heard it in a basement at six in the morning, television volume low because someone was still asleep upstairs. You heard it in a movie theater when a truck unfolded its chest and spoke without sounding like merchandise. You heard it when a stuffed donkey sighed in a way that made your own bad day feel officially registered.

Cullen was born Peter Claver Cullen in Montreal in 1941 and spent more than six decades making other people's characters sound like they had weight. Voice acting is often treated as the invisible trade. Cullen treated it like carpentry. Every line fitted flush.

The resume confuses strangers on purpose. Optimus Prime. Eeyore. Monterey Jack. The first KARR on Knight Rider. The clicking breath in the Predator's jungle. Thaedus in Invincible. A career that runs from cereal commercials to prestige animation without ever sounding like he was slumming or showing off.

People love to point out that the same larynx produced Earth's most dependable commander and its most committed pessimist. The better observation is that both sounded honest. Optimus did not bark orders for the thrill of it. Eeyore did not perform sadness for laughs. Cullen gave each character a center of gravity and then refused to wobble.

He found Prime's voice in an unlikely place: his older brother Larry, a Marine home on leave. Cullen wanted power. Larry told him a real leader does not need to shout. So the Autobot who could bench-press a building talked like a man checking that everyone had made it off the field.

That choice mattered more than any toy aisle ever admitted. A lot of eighties action heroes sounded like they were selling aggression. Prime sounded like he was asking you to stand up straight because someone smaller was counting on you. Kids absorbed that before they had vocabulary for it.

When the Transformers franchise kept rebooting, recasting, and rebooting again, the one constant studios kept returning to was Cullen's voice - as if the character were incomplete without the original timber. They do not do that for nostalgia alone. They do it because audiences notice absence the way they notice a missing step on a staircase.

Cullen understood the contract between a voice actor and a fanbase that grows up. At conventions he signed autographs for hours. He listened to people describe what a single line meant to them on the worst day of their life. He did not treat those stories as embarrassing. He treated them as the point.

His family asked the public to honor him by serving their communities with compassion, integrity, and loyalty, and to remember what he liked to say: be strong enough to be gentle. Memorial services will be private. Organizers have requested that guests not treat the parking lot like a launch bay. Several mourners said they understood.

There will be tributes. There will be clip reels. Someone on social media will post the transformation sound effect with a crying emoji and mean it completely.

What will linger is simpler: a generation learned what leadership sounded like before they learned what leadership cost. They learned it from a Canadian actor in a recording booth who never needed the camera to find him.

Till all are one, Mr. Cullen. The rest of us are still learning the lines you left behind.
