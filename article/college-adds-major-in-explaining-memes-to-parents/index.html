College Adds Major in Explaining Memes to Parents

Degree track covers tone, context, and when not to show them anything.

By Dr. Helen Marsh · Wednesday, August 19, 2026 · ANN ARBOR · 2 min read

Share Email Post Reprints

ANN ARBOR - The University of Michigan has taken a bold step in higher education by launching an entirely new major: B.A. in Intergenerational Memetics, set to debut during spring semester. This unique program aims to prepare graduates to bridge the digital divide between millennials and their parents, ensuring that every family can enjoy internet humor without the awkward moments of miscommunication.

Dean Stephen Voss explained the rationale behind the curriculum. "Every company needs someone who can translate the nuances of internet culture for their executives," he said. "It's not just about explaining memes; it’s about understanding the context and delivering a message that resonates with all generations."

The core courses are designed to equip students with essential skills. "Context Collapse 101" teaches how to navigate the shifting landscape of online communication, while "When to Lie and Say You Saw It Live" offers practical advice on maintaining family harmony. The capstone project requires students to successfully explain a trending video to a panel of actors portraying concerned aunts, ensuring they can handle any situation that might arise.

Parents were naturally curious about the return on investment (ROI) for this unconventional degree. Voss cited high starting salaries at media firms, political campaigns, and any office with Slack as evidence of its value. "We're not just preparing students to explain memes," he said. "We’re training them in critical communication skills that are highly sought after."

Scholarships are available for first-generation meme explainers from households that still forward chain emails. Dean Voss emphasized the importance of supporting these students, stating, "These individuals have a unique perspective and can make a significant impact on their communities."

The accreditation process is currently underway, with a board that requested the syllabus be sent as a PDF, not a TikTok. This detail highlights the ongoing tension between traditional academic standards and modern digital culture.

Parent orientation will include a breakout session on why thumbs-up reactions are not endorsements of life choices. "We want to ensure parents understand the nuances," said Voss. "A simple thumbs-up can mean anything from 'I agree' to 'I don't know what you're talking about.'"

Graduates will receive a diploma and a laminated card reading, "Just Show Them the Dog Video Instead." This humorous detail encapsulates the broader mission of the program: to help parents appreciate internet culture without the need for complicated explanations.

Owen Kessler, one of the first students to enroll in the B.A. in Intergenerational Memetics, expressed his excitement about the opportunity. "My mom thinks LOL means lots of love," he said with a laugh. "This is a calling."

The University of Michigan's innovative approach to education reflects its commitment to preparing students for an increasingly digital world. As Dean Voss put it, "We’re not just teaching memes; we’re teaching communication in the 21st century."
