Silly Scientists Discover New Planet

Team best known for sending a toaster into orbit claims to have found Zoogon, a world of sentient French-speaking trees.

By Celeste Marlow · Friday, January 16, 2026 · PASADENA · 3 min read

Share Email Post Reprints

PASADENA - Astronomers at the Eccentric Research Collective announced the discovery of Zoogon, a planet they describe as "Earth-adjacent in mass and France-adjacent in attitude," orbiting a star visible only when peer reviewers look away.

Team leader Dr. Felix Wren said that while the surface of Zoogon is covered in sentient trees communicating exclusively in fluent French and refusing to discuss the weather, his team managed to intercept a signal that translated to "Your telescope is rude." "We're not sure if it's a diplomatic message or just a bit of cosmic static," Wren admitted. The claim rests on three observations: a blurry photograph, a dream journal shared by six graduate students, and a spectrograph reading that may also be a coffee stain.

NASA congratulated the team on their enthusiasm while noting that official exoplanet confirmation requires data beyond "vibes and a corkboard with red string." Wren, whose prior work includes measuring how fast a rubber duck spins in zero gravity, dismissed skepticism. "Every breakthrough looks silly until it has a gift shop," he said. "And Zoogon is already inspiring t-shirts."

The International Astronomical Union scheduled a hearing next month to discuss the validity of the discovery. Wren plans to attend with slides, snacks, and a tree costume he insists is "method research." He also proposed launching a probe carrying baguettes "for diplomatic purposes," though funding for this mission remains uncertain.

Local planetariums reported increased ticket sales from families who "just want to see what nonsense looks like with a laser show." One parent commented, "It's great for the kids to see that scientists can be as silly as their favorite TV shows."

The discovery has sparked debate among botanists. Dr. Eliza Bonsai of the Botanical Society of America asked whether the trees on Zoogon photosynthesize or merely critique sunlight. "If they're critiquing, it could mean they have a form of consciousness," she said.

Wren's team is currently working on a follow-up mission to gather more data. They plan to send a probe equipped with a high-resolution camera and a portable Wi-Fi router to establish a tree-to-tree network. "We need to understand their social structure before we can fully communicate," Wren explained.

Meanwhile, the team has set up a website where visitors can submit their own interpretations of the spectrograph reading. One visitor wrote, "I think it says 'You're not as smart as you think,' which is pretty accurate."

Funding for the follow-up mission is pending, but Wren remains optimistic. He said, "We have to believe in something bigger than ourselves, even if that something is a planet full of trees that can't stop talking about how bad our telescopes are."

Zoogon has not returned messages. Wren called that "very French," and added with a shrug, "Maybe they're just having a quiet day."

The team's next step will be to analyze the dream journal entries from the graduate students. According to one entry, "I dreamed of a planet where trees argue about the weather all night." Another wrote, "The trees are making fun of my telescope again."

As the Eccentric Research Collective awaits further confirmation, they remain undeterred by skepticism. Wren said, "Every breakthrough looks silly until it has a gift shop. And Zoogon is already inspiring t-shirts."

The future of exoplanet research may just depend on how well we can communicate with trees that refuse to discuss the weather.
