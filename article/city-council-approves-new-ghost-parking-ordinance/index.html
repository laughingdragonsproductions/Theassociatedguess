City Council Approves New Ghost Parking Ordinance

Designated apparition stalls will reduce driveway haunts and meter disputes, officials say, though enforcement remains largely faith-based.

By Tomás Reyes · Friday, January 2, 2026 · MERIDIAN · 3 min read

Share Email Post Reprints

MERIDIAN - The City Council voted 7 - 1 Tuesday to establish the nation's first municipal ghost parking program, creating twelve designated stalls where spirits may leave their apparitions without blocking driveways, bike lanes, or community theater fundraisers. The move comes after a year of escalating complaints about "unauthorized ectoplasmic storage," particularly in historic districts where ghosts prefer Victorian architecture and parallel parking.

Transportation Director Gail Pomeroy explained the rationale behind the new ordinance during Tuesday's council meeting. "We've seen an increase of 40% in ghost-related disturbances," she said, her voice tinged with a mix of exasperation and resolve. "These aren't just fleeting apparitions; they're causing real disruptions to our community."

Each stall will be marked with reflective paint visible only on foggy nights, ensuring that only the most dedicated - or perhaps the most insubstantial - ghosts can claim these spots. The stalls are equipped with a timer that resets when the entity achieves closure, presumably after resolving whatever unfinished business they may have had in life.

Council member Harold Finch cast the lone no vote, arguing the city should invest in living residents first. "My constituents cannot find parking after 6 p.m., " he said. "Now we are reserving spaces for people who do not pay property tax because they predate the assessor's office."

Pomeroy countered that ghost parking fees - collected in vintage coins and unresolved emotional debt - are projected to fund two crosswalk signals and a public fountain that weeps on anniversaries. "We're turning a potential nuisance into an opportunity," she said, her eyes twinkling with a hint of mischief.

Meridian's Ghost Relations Office will issue translucent permits after a brief interview confirming the applicant is deceased and not merely "very tired." Commercial hauntings require additional liability insurance to ensure that businesses are fully prepared for any spectral visitors. "We don't want any accidental poltergeist parties," Pomeroy said, her tone light but serious.

Local business owners expressed cautious optimism. Café owner Lena Ortiz said phantom double-parking had cost her a delivery ramp. "If they have a spot, maybe they stop hovering near the espresso machine," she said with a wry smile. "And that would be a relief."

The ordinance takes effect next month, but until then, spirits may continue using existing rules: first come, first scream. Mayor Walter M. Wraith (no relation) added, "We'll see how it goes. If the ghosts don't like their new spots, they can always haunt the library - there's plenty of room there."

As the news spread, residents began to grapple with the implications. Some saw it as a practical solution to an age-old problem, while others wondered if this was just another step in Meridian's long and storied history of ghostly encounters.

In the meantime, the city has set up a hotline for residents to report any disturbances or concerns about their new ghost neighbors. "We want everyone to feel safe," said Councilman Finch, his voice tinged with a hint of resignation. "But I'm not sure how many people will call when they can't even find parking."

The ordinance is expected to be a boon for local businesses and a source of ongoing humor for the community. As one resident put it, "At least now we know where to look if we need a quick getaway from a particularly persistent poltergeist."
