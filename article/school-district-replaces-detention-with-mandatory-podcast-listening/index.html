School District Replaces Detention With Mandatory Podcast Listening

Officials say fifteen minutes of true crime 'builds empathy and better headphones hygiene.

By Claire Undertow · Wednesday, August 19, 2026 · MAPLE RIDGE · 3 min read

Share Email Post Reprints

MAPLE RIDGE - Starting this fall, students who disrupt class will serve Podcast Reflection instead of detention, a program superintendent Dana Hooper described as "restorative, educational, and slightly terrifying in a wholesome way."

"Writing 'I will not throw grapes' fifty times didn't work," Hooper said. "But hearing a calm narrator describe forensic accounting? That builds character." The new system aims to provide students with a more engaging form of discipline while also offering them valuable life lessons.

The curriculum for Podcast Reflection is carefully curated, ranging from etiquette experts discussing the proper way to hold a wine glass to episodes about people who lied on resumes and got caught at the company picnic. "We're not just talking about passive listening," Hooper explained. "These are real-world scenarios that can help students understand the consequences of their actions."

Offenders receive noise-canceling earbuds and a playlist tailored to their level of offense. Minor infractions, such as whispering during class or leaving homework on a desk, result in a selection from the introductory playlist. More serious offenses, like throwing grapes (a long-standing tradition in Maple Ridge), lead to episodes about people who have faced similar challenges.

Repeat incidents escalate to documentary series about bridge maintenance, which staff call "the nuclear option." Hooper noted that these documentaries are particularly effective because they require students to focus on the content and understand the importance of infrastructure. "It's like a mini-lesson in civic responsibility," she said with a dry smile.

Parents were split on the new policy. "My son finally learned what a Roth IRA is," said Elena Platt, a local parent who was pleasantly surprised by her child’s newfound knowledge. "He came home and started asking me about retirement accounts." However, others worried about age-appropriate content. The district promised filters, though IT director Gil Marr admitted, "one kid already found a show about competitive cheese."

Teachers report improved hallway behavior and a 22% increase in students saying "interesting" without meaning it. Hooper said, "It's not just about discipline; it's about creating an environment where students are engaged and learning." Critics note the policy outsources discipline to advertisers, but Hooper defended the approach by pointing out that many of the shows are educational and provide valuable life lessons.

The district has ordered forty charging stations to ensure that all students have access to their earbuds. Additionally, they have hired a therapist who specializes in parasocial relationships to help students navigate the emotional impact of listening to strangers on the internet. "We want to make sure this is a positive experience for everyone," Hooper said.

Hooper also mentioned that traditional detention may return if podcast hosts unionize. Until then, the district is committed to its new approach. "We're not just replacing one form of discipline with another; we're providing students with a more engaging and educational experience," she concluded.

As the school year begins, Maple Ridge parents will be watching closely to see how this new policy plays out. For now, the district remains optimistic that Podcast Reflection will become an integral part of their disciplinary strategy.
