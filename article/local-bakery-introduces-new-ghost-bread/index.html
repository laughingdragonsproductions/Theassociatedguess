Local Bakery Introduces New Ghost Bread

After months of midnight complaints, Hartwell's Crumb & Specter releases a loaf formulated for entities that cannot hold utensils.

By Mae Holloway · Friday, February 27, 2026 · HARTWELL · 3 min read

Share Email Post Reprints

HARTWELL - Hartwell's Crumb & Specter Bakery unveiled Ghost Bread on Thursday, a sourdough variant engineered for patrons who arrive after closing, pass through walls, and have historically left flour handprints on ceilings.

Owner Denise Korr said the recipe began as damage control. "We tried signs. We tried holy water near the register. The ghosts kept eating our day-old rye and leaving reviews that only dogs could hear." She added, with a sigh, "They even left notes in invisible ink. 'You should try to make it better.' That's what they wrote."

The loaf uses a low-density crumb, trace amounts of ectoplasm-neutral salt, and a crust designed to audibly crunch for beings without corporeal ears. Focus groups of local mediums rated the texture "present" and the aroma "like a memory of wheat." Korr explained that the bread was not just an experiment but a necessity. "We can't keep losing our best-selling rye loaf," she said, her voice tinged with frustration.

City health inspectors approved the product under a provisional category for "non-corporeal food service," provided the bakery maintains separate tongs and does not claim the bread cures unfinished business. Inspector Mark Thompson, who specializes in ghostly affairs, noted, "We're keeping an eye on this one. If it starts to produce any corporeal effects, we'll have to revisit our classification."

Paranormal economist Dr. Lyle Penn noted Hartwell's ghost population has risen 12% since the pandemic, when many residents simply stopped leaving their houses and, according to municipal records, their bodies. "With more ghosts around, there's a growing market for non-corporeal goods," said Penn, who specializes in the economic impact of the afterlife.

Korr said early sales exceeded projections after a viral video showed a translucent figure in a top hat purchasing four loaves with exact change that materialized from nowhere. Skeptics called it a marketing stunt involving fishing line and a cousin in accounting. "We tried to make it look like a trick, but the ghosts are too smart for that," Korr admitted.

The bakery will offer Ghost Bread Thursdays alongside its regular menu. Gluten-free and vegan options remain unavailable to the deceased, who Korr said "are already on the strictest diet imaginable." She explained, "They don't have access to many of our ingredients. They can't digest them."

Neighboring businesses welcomed the innovation. The laundromat next door reported fewer floating socks. Manager Sarah Johnson said, "It's a relief not to find laundry in the air anymore. It's like they're finally cleaning up after themselves." The funeral home across the street declined comment but extended hours.

Korr invited skeptics to visit after midnight. "Bring appetite or bring salt," she said with a wry smile. "We can work with either."

Local historian Dr. Eliza Hartwell, no relation to the bakery owner, commented on the trend of ghostly consumption in town. "Hartwell has always had its share of spirits, but this seems like an unprecedented level of activity." She added, "It's fascinating how businesses are adapting to cater to these... less tangible customers."

As night fell over Hartwell, Crumb & Specter Bakery remained open late, the scent of Ghost Bread wafting through the air. Patrons who were not ghosts could be seen lingering outside, perhaps hoping for a glimpse of what lay inside. Korr herself was busy preparing more loaves, her hands moving with practiced ease as she spoke to the unseen.

"Who knows? Maybe one day we'll have an entire menu for the afterlife," she mused aloud. "Until then, Ghost Bread will do."
